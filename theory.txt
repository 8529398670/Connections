We’ve been getting a lot of questions about how to get better at Connections, the newest game in the New York Times Games catalog. While there’s not a single best strategy, we’ve had a lot of fun over the past few months developing our own approaches. We spoke to a few experienced solvers and the game’s editors to get some tips.

First, make sure you familiarize yourself with the rules, which you can find by clicking or tapping the question mark icon in the top right corner of the game.

Ultimately, your gameplay is going to be informed by your unique knowledge and personality. Don’t let the pursuit of a “perfect” solve stand in the way of having a good time and solving the puzzle.

Have a Plan
We don’t expect you to know every possible way a category can come together, but it’s helpful to know the most common ways that they’re structured. The categories have set difficulties. Yellow is the easiest, then green, then blue. Purple is the trickiest. Typically, categories involve trivia or the meaning or structure of words.

Knowing too much can send you in the wrong direction, though. “This is a game where if you have too much knowledge, it can hurt you,” Regina Bediako, a solver from Oakland, Calif., said.

Wyna Liu, a puzzle editor who creates the game boards, says that what makes the purple category hard is the abstractness of the links between the terms. Overlap with other categories can also make it difficult.

Joel Fagliano, a senior puzzle editor, tests and edits the boards after Ms. Liu compiles them. He starts each solve by looking for terms with a single possible definition, he said. A word like “train” could mean many different things, but a word like “metaverse” could only mean one.

You can use context clues to learn more. For example, “baloney” could refer to the food item or to nonsense, but if there are no other food items on the board, it’s clear that it can only have one meaning.

It’s also worth repeating some advice from Getting to Genius here: It’s important to set a reasonable and clear goal for yourself, and to understand that progress toward that goal might be slow at first. If you solve as often as you can, you’ll slowly get better at the game.

The First Guess Is the Hardest
Learning to identify a puzzle’s difficulty at first glance is incredibly helpful. Remember, the terms are all members of the same category, not necessarily words that mean the same thing.

The purple category, which is the hardest one, can contain tricky rhymes, homophones, anagrams, palindromes, unmarked fill-in-the-blank phrases or total wild cards.

“I’ll try to look at patterns of what Connections has done in the past,” said Morgan Payne of Sterling, Va., who regularly solves Connections for her followers on TikTok. Ms. Payne recognizes a few common categories from past experience: word and letter patterns, pop culture clues (such as music and movie titles) and fill-in-the-blank phrases.

The difficulty of the game is subjective, Ms. Liu said, and what you know might not always line up with what’s in the puzzle that day. She often sees this in the feedback she gets from members of the puzzle’s testing panel, who are paid to check each puzzle for difficulty, bugs, inconsistencies and any other issues, she added.

Watch Out for Overlap
Spotting a red herring, or an entry that looks as if it fits into one category but really goes in another, is one of the toughest skills to learn. It’s a key thing the puzzle’s editors look for: “More overlap makes a harder puzzle,” Mr. Fagliano said.

He reiterated his advice about looking for terms with a single definition, and added that once he has selected the entries for his guess, he looks around the board to make sure there aren’t any more that can fit into the category.

If you see many tiles that could fit into a single category, try not to use your guesses to make it work. Ms. Liu said you should save your attempts for a category that has the fewest possible potential items.

This is also the place where you can best use your crossword knowledge to try and make some progress. “Synonyms are often clues for each other in the crossword,” Mr. Fagliano said. But be prepared to trek other paths, he said, as this won’t save you every day. “‘Things that whistle’ isn’t a crossword thing at all,” he said, “just something clever that Wyna came up with.”

Ms. Liu’s main advice? “Eliminate options. Clear the board as much as possible.”

Use Your Resources
The game itself gives you plenty of hints if you’re paying attention.

“When you start the game, go as long as you can without making a guess,” Ms. Liu said. Even so, she said, it’s best to use a guess if you’re totally stumped. If you’re off by one term in a given category, the game will give you the hint “One away!” Remember those guesses so you can figure out which term doesn’t fit with the others.

Ms. Payne said she often played a guessing game when she was “one away.” She will single out a word and figure out how it might fit into other categories on the board, even if it means using up her attempts.

You can also lean on your expertise. Ms. Bediako said her strength was figuring out pop-culture categories.

“If I see a proper noun up in the mix, it’s like Christmas for me,” she said.
